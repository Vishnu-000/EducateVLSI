DAY 22
Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS)
Every flip-flop in your design must receive the clock signal at almost exactly the same moment. CTS builds the buffer tree that makes this happen. Clock skew — the difference in arrival times across the chip — is the PD parameter that determines if you can meet timing at all. Master CTS and you master half of timing closure.
~
Why Clock Tree Synthesis Is Necessary
THE PROBLEM
One driver cannot reach 100,000 FFs
A clock signal starts at one source (PLL output or I/O pin). It must reach every flip-flop — potentially 50,000–500,000 of them. Wire resistance means the last FF sees the clock 10–50 ns later than the first. This clock skew destroys timing.
THE SOLUTION
A balanced buffer tree
CTS inserts clock buffers (CLKBUF) in a tree structure — like a tournament bracket in reverse. Each level fans out to more buffers. The tree is balanced so every leaf (FF clock pin) has equal total path delay from the clock source.
CLOCK SKEW
Difference in clock arrival times
Skew = max(arrival_time) - min(arrival_time) across all clock sinks. Target: <50–100ps for typical designs, <20ps for high-frequency. Large skew = setup violations on one side, hold violations on the other.
INSERTION DELAY
Clock latency from source to sinks
The time for the clock to travel from source to all FFs. Typically 0.5–2ns. Must be accounted for in STA — the clock arrives at FFs later than at the source. SDC command: set_clock_latency is set automatically post-CTS.
Memory Analogy
CTS = a fire drill with a perfect alarm system. Every classroom (flip-flop) must hear the alarm (clock edge) at the same moment. Without CTS, the alarm rings in classroom 1 first — those students line up while classroom 100 is still seated. Chaos (setup violations near the source, hold violations far away). CTS adds speaker amplifiers (clock buffers) in hallways so the sound reaches every room simultaneously — balanced tree = equal propagation delay everywhere.
⚙
CTS Targets — What You Configure and Why
Target skew Maximum allowed skew across all sinks on this clock domain. Typical: 50–100ps for 28nm, 100–200ps for 130nm. Tighter skew = more buffers = more area/power.
SDC: set_clock_uncertainty [expr 0.05 + $skew_budget] — skew is added to setup/hold uncertainty margins
Max clock buffer fanout Maximum number of sinks each clock buffer can drive. Typically 8–32. Too many = high load, slower edges, more skew. Too few = more levels = more insertion delay.
Innovus/OpenROAD: set_cts_max_fanout 16 — affects tree depth and total buffer count
Max cap / max slew Max capacitive load and max transition time allowed at each buffer output. From PDK constraints. Violations here = slow clock edges = timing problems and signal integrity issues.
Library provides max_cap and max_transition per cell pin — CTS checks these automatically
Clock buffer cell list Which cell types CTS is allowed to use: CLKBUF_X2, CLKBUF_X4, CLKBUF_X8, CLKINV_X2... Only clock-optimised cells should be in this list — not regular BUF cells (different drive strength characteristics).
OpenROAD: set_cts_buffer_list {CLKBUF_4 CLKBUF_8 CLKBUF_16} — critical for power-efficient CTS
NDR (Non-Default Rules) Clock nets are routed with double-width wires and double-spacing. This reduces resistance (less insertion delay) and improves signal integrity (less crosstalk noise on the clock). Must be defined before CTS routing.
Innovus: setNDRRule -name CTS_NDR -spacing 2x -width 2x ; then assign to clock nets
⊞
Critical CTS Concepts — Interview Topics
USEFUL SKEW
Intentional skew to fix hold
On a path with a hold violation, you can intentionally delay the clock to the capture FF — making it arrive later — which relaxes the hold check. This is called "useful skew" or "local skew adjustment." Powerful but must be done carefully to avoid setup violations elsewhere.
HOLD VIOLATIONS AFTER CTS
CTS changes hold margins
Before CTS, all FFs see the clock simultaneously (ideal clock). After CTS, real skew means some FFs see clock earlier or later. Paths where capture FF is faster than launch FF now have tighter hold margins — and may violate. Expect hold fixups after CTS.
MULTI-CLOCK DESIGNS
Separate trees per clock domain
Each independent clock (clk_core, clk_usb, clk_pcie) gets its own CTS tree. Paths crossing clock domains (CDC paths) are handled with set_false_path or set_max_delay -datapath_only in the SDC — CTS does not balance these.
CLOCK GATING
Power-saving CTS optimization
Integrated Clock Gating (ICG) cells sit in the clock tree and can disable the clock for sections of the design when idle — reducing dynamic power by 20–40%. CTS must place ICG cells close to the FFs they gate to minimise skew impact.

DAY 23
Routing — Connecting Every Net with Metal
Routing draws the actual metal wires that connect every placed cell, clock buffer, and I/O pin according to the netlist. It must do this on multiple metal layers, obey thousands of design rules, meet timing, and achieve zero DRC violations. A clean route is the culmination of every decision made in the flow so far.
⊟
Metal Layer Stackup — The Routing Highway System
M8–M9
H/V
Power stripes, clock trunks · Lowest R/μm · Widest pitch
M5–M7
H/V
Long data wires, clock distribution · Medium pitch
M3–M4
H/V
Standard signal routing · Alternating H/V · Medium R
M2
H
Local signal routing · Dense · High R/μm
M1
V
Power rails only (VDD/VSS) · Standard cell internal
H/V ALTERNATION
Prevent parallel wire crosstalk
Even layers route horizontal, odd layers vertical (or vice versa — PDK-defined). Adjacent parallel wires on the same layer create capacitive crosstalk. Alternating layers means most crossing wires are perpendicular — minimal crosstalk.
VIAS
Layer-to-layer connections
When a wire must change metal layers, a via (a vertical metal plug) connects M2 to M3, M3 to M4, etc. Vias add resistance and area. Minimising via count improves timing and reduces DRC risk. DRC often flags incorrectly stacked vias.
ROUTING TRACKS
Where wires are allowed to go
Each layer has a fixed grid of routing tracks defined by the PDK (track pitch). A wire must run exactly on a track. More tracks = more routing capacity. Running out of tracks = routing overflow.
NDR FOR CLOCKS
Double-width, double-spacing
Clock nets use Non-Default Rules: 2× wire width (lower resistance = less insertion delay variation) and 2× spacing (less coupling capacitance from adjacent signal nets = less crosstalk glitches on the clock).
→
Two-Phase Routing Process
PHASE 1 — GLOBAL ROUTING
Plan the path, not the wire
Divides the chip into a coarse grid of GCells. For each net, finds the sequence of GCells it passes through — a high-level route plan. Does not draw actual wires. Reports congestion: how many nets need to pass through each GCell vs how many tracks are available. Overflow GCells must be fixed before detail routing.
PHASE 2 — DETAIL ROUTING
Draw the actual DRC-clean wires
Takes global route plan and draws specific metal segments on specific tracks and layers. Must satisfy all DRC rules simultaneously: min spacing, min width, min enclosure, via rules, antenna rules. Uses maze routing algorithms. Any remaining DRC violations are flagged as errors.
Min spacingMinimum distance between two parallel wires on the same layer. Violation = shorts risk during manufacturing.
Most common DRC violation. Caused by the router being forced into a congested area.
Min widthMinimum wire width on each layer. Too-thin wire → higher resistance → timing violation AND electromigration risk.
Min enclosureA via must be fully enclosed by metal on both layers it connects. If metal is too short, the via is not properly enclosed — process variation causes open circuit.
Antenna ruleDuring manufacturing, a long metal wire acts as an antenna and accumulates charge that can destroy the gate oxide of a transistor. Long nets require antenna diodes or jumper vias to protect gates.
Antenna violations checked separately by Calibre antenna tool. Fixed by routing engineer inserting diode cells.
Off-gridWire not aligned to the routing track grid. Causes mis-alignment with adjacent cells and via misalignment. Always caused by manual edits or ECO changes.

DAY 24
Static Timing Analysis & Signoff
STA is the mathematical proof that your chip will work at the required clock frequency across all process, voltage, and temperature corners. Signoff is the complete checklist — DRC, LVS, STA, antenna, ERC — that a chip must pass before the GDSII file is sent to the foundry. Nothing ships without clean signoff.
⏱
Static Timing Analysis — How It Actually Works
WHAT STA DOES
Exhaustively checks every path
STA analyses every combinational path from every flip-flop output to every flip-flop input — potentially millions of paths — and checks that data arrival ≤ data required time. No simulation needed. It is exhaustive and deterministic.
PVT CORNERS
Process·Voltage·Temperature
Silicon varies during manufacturing (Process: slow/fast/typical). Supply voltage varies (±10%). Temperature varies (−40°C to 125°C). STA runs at multiple corners to ensure the chip works in the worst case for both setup and hold.
SETUP CORNER
Worst-case slow (SS, 0.9V, 125°C)
Slow process + low voltage + high temperature = slowest possible logic. Setup violations here mean the chip fails at speed in the worst production unit. This is your setup timing sign-off corner.
HOLD CORNER
Worst-case fast (FF, 1.1V, −40°C)
Fast process + high voltage + low temperature = fastest possible logic. Hold violations here mean data races to the capture FF and corrupts it. Hold must be clean at the fast corner — the exact opposite of setup.
MCMM
Multi-Corner Multi-Mode
A single chip may operate in multiple modes (functional, test, low-power) and must be signed off at multiple corners simultaneously. Modern STA runs 6–20 scenarios (corner × mode). All must have zero violations at tapeout.
CPPR
Clock Path Pessimism Removal
STA applies pessimism to both launch and capture clock paths independently — this double-counts the shared clock path pessimism. CPPR removes this artificial pessimism, giving a more accurate (slightly better) slack. Always enable CPPR in signoff STA.

Complete Signoff Checklist — Every Item Must Pass
DRC — Design Rule Check
Zero physical design rule violations
Run by Calibre DRC. Checks min spacing, min width, enclosure, via rules, density rules (metal fill), well spacing, antenna rules. Every foundry rule must pass. Even 1 DRC violation = chip cannot be fabricated correctly.
LVS — Layout vs Schematic
Physical layout matches gate netlist
Run by Calibre LVS. Extracts the circuit from the GDS layout and compares it to the Verilog netlist. Every net connection must match exactly. A short (two nets merged) or open (net broken) is a fatal LVS error — the chip will be functionally wrong.
STA — Setup Timing (All corners)
WNS ≥ 0 at slow corner (SS, 0.9V, 125°C)
Run by PrimeTime. Every timing path must have non-negative setup slack at the slow-slow process corner, lowest operating voltage, highest temperature. This is the hardest timing check. Zero violations required.
STA — Hold Timing (All corners)
Hold slack ≥ 0 at fast corner (FF, 1.1V, −40°C)
Every path must have non-negative hold slack at the fast-fast process corner. Hold violations cause functional failures even at low frequency. Every hold violation must be fixed with delay buffers before tapeout — no exceptions.
Antenna Check
No antenna ratio violations on any net
Metal area accumulated during manufacturing cannot exceed the foundry's antenna ratio limit relative to the gate oxide area. Violations damage gates permanently during manufacturing. Fixed by inserting antenna diode cells or adding via jumpers (routing the wire to a higher layer earlier).
ERC — Electrical Rule Check
No electromigration or max capacitance violations
Current through any wire must not exceed the electromigration limit (mA/μm per layer). No net may exceed max_capacitance (causes slow transitions). No cell output may exceed max_fanout. ERC violations cause premature chip failure in the field.
Metal Fill / Density
Metal layer density within min/max bounds
Foundries require each metal layer to have density between ~20% and 80% across every region. Too sparse = CMP (Chemical Mechanical Polishing) dishing. Too dense = bridging risk. Fill cells (dummy metal) are inserted automatically by the tool to meet density rules.
IR Drop Analysis
IR drop < 10% of VDD at every cell
Power integrity analysis (Redhawk, Voltus, or OpenROAD). Every cell must receive adequate supply voltage under worst-case switching conditions. IR drop > 10% = cells run too slow = timing fails in silicon even if STA passed at nominal voltage.
⚠
Tapeout is irreversible. Once the GDSII file is sent to the foundry and masks are made, there is no going back. A mask set costs $500,000–$15,000,000 depending on the process node. Fabrication takes 8–16 weeks. Every item on this checklist must pass before tapeout — not 99%, not 99.9%. Zero violations. This is why PD engineers are so rigorous.

Phase 2 Final Assessment — All 10 Days
10 questions covering the complete Phase 2 curriculum from CMOS transistors to signoff. These are real interview questions asked by companies like Qualcomm, MediaTek, Apple, and Intel for PD engineer roles.
Q1. What is clock skew and why does it create both setup AND hold problems simultaneously?
Skew = max(arrival) - min(arrival). For a launch→capture FF path: if the capture FF's clock arrives LATER than the launch FF (positive skew at capture) → the capture window opens later → less time for data to arrive → setup gets HARDER. If the capture clock arrives EARLIER (negative skew at capture) → the capture window closes before data has propagated enough → hold gets HARDER. Large skew means some paths suffer setup and other paths suffer hold — simultaneously.
Q2. Why are clock nets routed with Non-Default Rules (NDR) — specifically double-width and double-spacing?
Double-width clock wires have lower resistance per unit length (R=ρL/A — larger A = lower R). Lower R means less RC delay variation across different wire lengths in the tree, which means all FF clock pins see more similar insertion delays → lower skew. Double-spacing reduces capacitive coupling to adjacent nets. If a nearby data net switches, it injects charge into the clock net through coupling capacitance — a glitch that can cause a false clock edge or timing uncertainty. Double-spacing reduces this coupling by 50%+.
Q3. Metal layers alternate between horizontal and vertical routing directions. What specific problem does this solve?
Parallel adjacent wires on the same layer have high capacitive coupling — proportional to the shared length. This creates crosstalk: a switching aggressor wire injects a voltage pulse onto a quiet victim wire, potentially causing a glitch or timing shift. Perpendicular wires have minimal coupling area (they cross at one point, not run alongside each other). Alternating H/V means a horizontal wire on M3 sits above/below vertical wires on M4 — perpendicular crossing = tiny coupling area = minimal crosstalk.
Q4. STA runs at "slow-slow (SS) corner" for setup and "fast-fast (FF) corner" for hold. Why are these different corners?
Setup violation = data arrives too LATE. Worst case = slowest logic = SS (slow-slow process, 0.9V, 125°C). If setup passes at SS, it passes everywhere because all other corners are faster. Hold violation = data arrives too EARLY. Worst case = fastest logic = FF (fast-fast process, 1.1V, -40°C). At FF corner, logic is fastest and data races to the capture FF quickest — if hold is satisfied here, it's satisfied everywhere. You check the extreme that makes each problem hardest.
Q5. What is the difference between DRC and LVS? A design with zero DRC errors can still fail LVS — give an example of why.
DRC = "does each shape obey the foundry's manufacturing rules?" (min width, min spacing, density, etc.) LVS = "does the physical circuit extracted from the layout match the intended schematic/netlist?" These check orthogonal things. Example of DRC-clean, LVS-fail: two nets (net_A and net_B) are accidentally connected by a stray via. Each individual metal shape obeys DRC rules (proper width and spacing to everything else). But the LVS extractor finds that net_A and net_B are shorted — this is an LVS "short" error. The chip would be functionally wrong even though the layout is geometrically legal.
Q6. After routing, you find 15 spacing DRC violations all clustered in one corner of the die. What is the root cause and what is the correct fix?
Clustered DRC violations in one area = routing congestion. The router ran out of available routing tracks (the routing supply was less than routing demand in that region) and was forced to violate spacing rules to complete connections. The root cause is too many cells packed into that area — which was set at placement time. Fix: go back to placement, add a placement blockage in that region to push cells away, reduce utilization, or move a macro that was blocking routing tracks. Trying to fix DRC violations at the routing level without fixing the underlying congestion is ineffective.
Q7. What is an antenna violation and at what point in the PD flow does it occur?
Antenna violations are a manufacturing problem, not a simulation problem. During IC fabrication, each metal layer is deposited and etched using plasma. While M3 is being etched, a long M3 wire may be electrically connected only to a transistor gate (the M4 connections haven't been made yet). The plasma charges this "antenna" and current flows into the gate oxide — destroying it if the charge/area ratio exceeds the foundry's limit. Fix options: (1) insert an antenna diode cell (provides a safe discharge path to diffusion), (2) "jump" the wire to a higher metal layer (M4+) earlier in the route — higher metal layers are deposited later, so the antenna is shorter when it's being etched.
Q8. A chip has passed DRC and LVS but fails in silicon at the target clock frequency. STA showed zero violations. What could explain this?
STA is a model — models can be wrong or incomplete. Common reasons: (1) IR drop: STA assumed VDD=1.8V, but actual VDD at the most active cells was 1.6V due to IR drop. Slower. (2) On-chip variation (OCV): different die regions have slightly different process corners. AOCV/POCV models account for this; basic OCV may not. (3) Clock jitter: real clock has cycle-to-cycle jitter that adds to setup uncertainty. If not modeled in SDC set_clock_uncertainty, paths appear to have more margin than they do. (4) Signal integrity: crosstalk can slow a victim net or create false glitches — SI-aware STA (ECSM, CCS models) catches this; basic STA may not. This is why advanced sign-off runs SI analysis separately.
