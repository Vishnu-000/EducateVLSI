DAY 18
Floorplanning — Foundations
Floorplanning is the most important step in PD. A good floorplan makes everything else easier. A bad floorplan — wrong die size, bad macro placement, poor I/O assignment — creates problems that cannot be fixed downstream. Decisions made here echo through the entire flow.
□
What is Floorplanning?
DEFINITION
Defining the physical canvas
Floorplanning determines: the die area (how big the chip is), the core area (where cells are placed), I/O pad locations (where signals enter/exit), and macro placement (where large blocks like SRAM, PLL, IP sit).
INPUT
What you need
Gate-level netlist (.v), SDC constraints, LEF files (cell abstracts), technology file (design rules), die size estimate from architecture, I/O pad list from package team.
OUTPUT
What you produce
A DEF file with: die boundary defined, placement rows created, macros placed and fixed, I/O pins assigned, power grid stripes planned. Feeds directly into placement.
WHY IT MATTERS
The foundation of timing closure
A macro placed in the wrong location adds 0.5–2ns of wire delay to every path through it. Multiply that by 1000 paths and you have an impossible timing closure problem that no amount of cell sizing will fix.

DIE BOUNDARY
The chip's outer edge
The rectangular boundary of the entire chip. Determined by package constraints and area budget. Usually sized to be an integer multiple of standard cell height × row count.
CORE AREA
Where logic lives
Inner rectangle where standard cells and macros are placed. Core_area = standard_cell_area / utilization. 70% utilization means 30% is white space for routing and buffers.
I/O RING
Between core and die edge
I/O pad cells and power pads live between the core and die edge. Signal assignment: clocks go at the top, data buses at the sides, power pads distributed uniformly.
MACROS
Fixed large blocks
SRAM, PLL, ADC, USB/DDR PHY — pre-designed blocks placed early and "fixed" (locked). Standard cells fill around them. Macro placement determines whether critical paths are short or long.
∑
Die Area Calculation — How PD Engineers Estimate
Cell areaSum of all standard cell areas from synthesis area report. Unit: μm². Reported per cell type and total.
Example: synthesis reports total_cell_area = 42,500 μm²
Utilization targetTypically 70–80% for standard cell designs. Lower = easier routing but larger/more expensive die. Higher = cheaper die but routing congestion and timing risk.
80%+ utilization = congestion risk. 60%- = wasteful area
Core area formulacore_area_μm² = cell_area / utilization_fraction
Example: 42,500 / 0.75 = 56,667 μm²
PD tool command: initialize_floorplan -utilization 0.75
Die side lengthside_μm = sqrt(core_area) (for square die)
Example: sqrt(56,667) ≈ 238 μm → die is ~238 × 238 μm
Adjust aspect ratio for macro placement constraints: tall SRAM → tall/thin die
Aspect ratiowidth:height ratio of the die. 1:1 is ideal. Forced to be non-square when I/O constraints or macro shapes require it.
Rule: keep aspect ratio between 0.5 and 2.0 for manageable routing
Memory Analogy
Floorplanning = designing a factory floor. The die boundary is the building walls. The core area is the factory floor. Macros are the heavy machinery (CNC machines, boilers) you place first because they can't move. Standard cells are the workers at benches placed around the machines. I/O pins are the loading docks at the edges. A good factory layout puts the workers who talk to each other close together — and that's exactly what timing-driven placement does next.

DAY 19
Power Planning — VDD/VSS Grid Design
Every transistor needs power. Power planning creates the metal infrastructure that delivers VDD and GND to every single cell in the design. A weak power grid causes IR drop — voltage droops that slow cells and cause timing violations or, worse, functional failures.
⚡
Power Grid Structure — Rings, Stripes, Rails
POWER RINGS
Outer loop around core
Wide metal loops (M5–M8) running around the entire core perimeter. VDD ring and VSS ring are separate. These are the main power collectors that feed the stripes.
POWER STRIPES
Mesh inside core
Wide metal wires crossing the core horizontally (one layer) and vertically (another layer). Alternate VDD/VSS stripes. Higher utilization = more stripes needed to reduce IR drop.
POWER RAILS
M1 in every cell row
Every standard cell row has M1 VDD and VSS rails running horizontally. These connect to standard cell power pins directly. The stripes above connect to these rails via vias.
WELL TAPS
Substrate connections
Special cells that connect the N-well to VDD and P-substrate to VSS. Required every 20–50μm to prevent latch-up. A missing well tap = potential chip destruction from parasitic SCR activation.
▼
IR Drop — Why Power Grid Quality Determines Timing
WHAT IS IR DROP
Voltage loss in the power grid
V = I × R. Every metal wire has resistance R. Current I flows through it to power cells. The voltage at the cell = VDD - (I × R_path). If IR drop is large, cells see reduced supply voltage and run slower.
EFFECT ON TIMING
Lower VDD = slower cells
A 10% IR drop (e.g. 1.8V → 1.62V) increases cell delay by ~15–20%. Your timing was designed for nominal VDD — IR drop adds delay that causes violations. The tool accounts for this in sign-off corners.
FIXES
More metal = less resistance
1. Add more/wider power stripes. 2. Use higher metal layers (lower resistance per μm). 3. Add decap cells (decoupling capacitors — buffer switching current). 4. Reduce logic density hotspots.
ELECTROMIGRATION (EM)
Current density limit
Too much current through a narrow wire permanently damages it over time (metal atoms migrate). Each metal layer has a maximum current capacity. PD tool checks EM during routing — violations must be fixed before tapeout.
Memory Analogy
Power planning = the city water supply system. The power ring is the main water main at the city edge. Stripes are the neighbourhood distribution pipes. Cell rails are the house water pipes. IR drop = pressure loss by the time water reaches the last house. Too thin pipes = low pressure = showers don't work (cells run slow). The solution: wider pipes (wider stripes), more pipes (more stripes), and storage tanks (decap cells to buffer demand spikes).

DAY 20
Placement — Assigning XY to Every Cell
Placement assigns an X,Y coordinate and orientation to every standard cell in the design. It is the PD step with the highest impact on both timing and routing success. Good placement = timing closure is achievable. Bad placement = months of rework.
⊡
Three Phases of Placement
PHASE 1 — GLOBAL
Rough placement for density
Divides the core into a grid of bins. Places cells into bins to achieve uniform density. No legal positions yet — cells may overlap. Goal: minimise total wirelength. Tool uses HPWL (half-perimeter wirelength) as the optimization metric.
PHASE 2 — LEGALIZATION
Snap to rows, remove overlaps
Moves cells to legal positions on placement rows (must align to row height and site width). Resolves overlaps while trying to minimise disruption to global placement quality. Cells must be within the core area boundary.
PHASE 3 — DETAIL
Local optimisation for timing
Fine-tuning: moves individual cells by small distances to improve timing on critical paths. Techniques: cell swapping, mirroring, incremental refinement. This is where timing-driven placement pays off.
TIMING-DRIVEN
Critical path awareness
Modern PD tools run timing analysis during placement and push cells on critical paths closer together. Cells with the worst slack are prioritized for proximity placement. Wire length on critical paths directly determines if timing closure is possible.
⊞
Placement Rules & Constraints
Standard cell rowsAll standard cells must align to rows. Row height = cell height (fixed by PDK). Cells cannot span rows or be placed between rows.
SKY130 standard cell height = 2.72μm. All cells are a multiple of this height.
Site widthThe minimum horizontal unit for cell placement. Cells are placed at integer multiples of the site width. SKY130 site width = 0.46μm.
Cell width must be integer multiple of site width — PDK guarantees this for all library cells
Row orientationAlternating rows are flipped (N, FS, N, FS...). Flipped rows share VDD/VSS rails with normal rows — this is how power rails run continuously across the chip without additional vias.
Critical: cells must match row orientation. Placing an "N" cell in an "FS" row = VDD/VSS short circuit
Density constraintEach bin of the floorplan must have cell density ≤ utilization target. High-density bins create routing congestion. The placer reports congestion maps.
Check congestion report after placement: any bin > 90% is a routing risk
Macro halosExclusion zones around macros where standard cells cannot be placed. Prevents routing congestion around macro boundaries.
Typical halo: 5–10μm around all sides of each macro. Add in floorplan before placement.
📊
Placement Quality Metrics — What to Check
WNS (Worst Negative Slack)The worst setup slack across all timing paths after placement. Should be negative but improving from pre-placement estimates. Target: WNS close to 0ns post-placement (some tools report -0.3 to -0.5 as acceptable pre-CTS).
TNS (Total Negative Slack)Sum of all negative slacks. Indicates total timing work remaining. Should reduce as you progress through CTS and routing. Large TNS = many paths need fixing.
Congestion map2D heatmap of routing demand vs supply per bin. Red areas = overflow (more routing needed than tracks available). Must fix before routing by spreading cells.
Command: report_design_area / reportCongestion. Red bins > 5% of area = routing likely to fail
Total wirelengthEstimated total wire length from placement. Lower = better timing and less routing resources needed. Minimising HPWL during global placement directly predicts routing success.
💡
Golden rule of placement: Fix timing post-placement, not post-routing. Every additional step after placement locks in more decisions. If WNS is -3ns after placement, it will be -3ns or worse after routing (wire parasitics add more delay). You must reach approximately WNS ≈ 0 before routing — or timing closure after routing is impossible.

Reading a DEF File — What Each Section Means
DIEAREADefines the die boundary rectangle in database units (usually nm). DIEAREA ( 0 0 ) ( 460000 460000 ) = 460μm × 460μm die.
UNITSUNITS DISTANCE MICRONS 1000 — all coordinates in the file are in units of (1/1000) microns = nanometers.
COMPONENTSLists every placed cell: name, library cell type, placement status (PLACED/FIXED), XY location, orientation. This is the output of placement.
NETSLists all nets with their connected pins. Contains the logical connectivity but not yet the physical routing (wires). Added by router.
PINSI/O pin definitions — name, direction (INPUT/OUTPUT), net, layer, and location on die boundary.
ROWSDefines placement rows: site name, origin, orientation, step, count. Rows are what standard cells snap to.
QUIZ
Part 2 Self-Assessment — Floorplan, Power, Placement
Test your understanding of the three PD steps most heavily tested in job interviews. Answer without looking — if you need to check, re-read that day's content.
Q1. Synthesis reports total cell area = 60,000 μm². You target 75% utilization with a square die. What is the approximate die side length?
Core area = cell_area / utilization = 60,000 / 0.75 = 80,000 μm². Die side (square) = √80,000 ≈ 283 μm. Remember: utilization is the fraction of core area USED by cells. So core must be bigger: if cells = 60,000 μm² and they fill 75%, total core = 60,000/0.75 = 80,000 μm².
Q2. What causes IR drop and what are TWO ways to reduce it in physical design?
IR drop = voltage loss due to V=I×R where I = switching current and R = metal wire resistance. Two fixes: (1) Add wider or more power stripes — reduces R, so for same current I the voltage drop I×R is smaller. (2) Add decap (decoupling capacitor) cells — they store charge locally and supply it during switching events, reducing instantaneous I. A third fix is reducing logic density hotspots to reduce local I.
Q3. After global placement, the congestion report shows 12% overflow in one corner of the die. What does this mean and how do you fix it?
Routing overflow = routing demand > routing supply in a region. The router has finite tracks on each metal layer. 12% overflow means the router cannot fit all required wires there — it will "overflow" (route outside the normal grid) causing DRC violations. Fix: reduce cell density in that region using placement blockages, move macros away, or reduce overall utilization target to give cells more space to spread out.
Q4. Why must standard cells be placed on placement rows and align to the site width grid?
Standard cells are designed so that when placed in rows: their VDD/VSS rails abut (connect) at the cell boundaries. The M1 VDD rail runs along the top of each cell; the M1 VSS rail along the bottom. When cells are in a row, these rails form a continuous line that the power stripes above connect to. Off-grid placement breaks this continuity — power pins float unconnected. Site alignment also ensures the diffusion, poly, and metal layers of adjacent cells line up precisely as designed.
Q5. The WNS after placement is -1.8ns. You proceed to routing anyway. After routing, the WNS is -2.3ns. Why did timing get worse, and what should you have done differently?
Pre-route timing uses estimated wire parasitics (based on placement proximity). Real routing adds actual wire resistance and capacitance — always larger than estimates. So a -1.8ns WNS pre-route typically becomes -2ns+ post-route. The rule: never proceed to routing with WNS worse than ~0ns. Fix placement violations first using repair_design, optimize_design, or cell sizing. Once in routing, your options for fixing timing are limited and expensive (ECOs, rerouting, disrupting other paths).
