Physical Design Mentorship — Session 6

Power Planning: Grid Architecture, IR Drop and Electromigration

Synthesis & SDC → Floorplanning → Power Planning → (next: Placement)

Why This Session Matters

Every transistor on the chip needs a clean supply. If the grid is weak, supply voltage sags at the cells (IR drop), cells slow down, timing fails in ways that no STA setup run predicted, and in the worst case wires fuse over time (electromigration). Power problems are also among the most expensive to fix late, because the grid occupies the top metal layers that everything else routes around.

TheoryDebugInterview Prep

1. The Power Delivery Path

Current flows from the package/bump or pad, through the top thick metals, down through vias to the lower metals, and finally into the standard-cell rails that touch the transistors. Every segment has resistance, so voltage drops along the way.

Layer of the Power GridCritical Physical Role
Pads / bumpsEntry points for VDD/VSS from the package
Power ringsWide metal loops around the core (or around macros) that collect current from pads and feed the stripes
Power stripes (straps)Vertical and horizontal wide wires across the core, on upper metals, forming the low-impedance mesh
Standard-cell rails (followpins)Thin M1 rails running along each cell row, feeding the cells directly
Via stacksConnect the layers; their resistance and cut count determine reliability

Mentor's mental model: think of it as a river system. Pads are the springs, rings and stripes are the main rivers and tributaries, the M1 rails are the irrigation channels. The narrow channels at the end carry the least current each, but they are the ones the cells actually drink from.

2. Core Theory

2.1 IR Drop

∑ Governing Formulation · IR Drop & Effective Supply
$$\Delta V_{\text{drop}} = I \times R_{\text{mesh}}$$ $$V_{\text{cell, effective}} = V_{\text{DD}} - \Delta V_{\text{IR, VDD}} - V_{\text{bounce, VSS}}$$
Timing Impact: Because gate propagation delay increases non-linearly as supply falls ($t_d \propto \frac{V_{DD}}{(V_{DD}-V_{th})^\alpha}$), cells in an IR-drop droop region suffer major unbudgeted setup failures.
Metric / AspectStatic IR DropDynamic IR Drop
Underlying physicsAverage switching current across cycles ($I_{\text{avg}}$)Instantaneous peak current during clock edges ($I_{\text{peak}}(t)$)
Mesh characteristics capturedPure resistive weakness of stripes and rails ($R$)Resistive + inductive ($L \frac{di}{dt}$) + capacitive ($C$) effects
Typical applicationEarly planning, width and pitch sizingSign-off verification, hot-spot debugging
Target sign-off budgetTypically < 2%–3% of nominal $V_{\text{DD}}$Typically < 5%–10% of nominal $V_{\text{DD}}$

2.2 Electromigration (EM)

High current density in a wire moves metal atoms over time via electron momentum transfer, creating voids (open circuits) or hillocks (whisker shorts). Wire reliability depends on current density, temperature, and wire width.

∑ Reliability Criterion · Black's EM Current Density
$$J = \frac{I}{W \times T_{\text{layer}}} \le J_{\text{max}}(T_{\text{junction}}, \text{Metal Layer})$$
Fixing Rules: Widen stripes, increase parallel straps, and replace single vias with dense multi-cut via arrays to disperse current.
  • Power/ground EM: DC (unidirectional) current, so the limit is stricter. Fix with wider wires, more vias, more parallel straps.
  • Signal EM: mostly AC with average and RMS limits; matters on high-activity or heavily loaded nets (clocks especially).
  • Vias are common EM weak points: single-via connections carrying high current are classic failures.

2.3 Power Estimation to Size the Grid

∑ Estimation Equation · Supply Grid Current Demand
$$I_{\text{total}} \approx \frac{P_{\text{total}}}{V_{\text{DD}}} \quad \implies \quad I_{\text{strap}} = \frac{I_{\text{total}}}{N_{\text{straps}}} \times K_{\text{hotspot}}$$
Margin factor ($K_{\text{hotspot}}$): Power is non-uniform across the core. Clock trees, datapaths, and SRAMs concentrate switching activity.

3. Grid Design Decisions

3.1 Layer selection

Use the top thick metals for rings and straps because they have the lowest resistance per length. Use alternating horizontal/vertical layers so they form a mesh. Keep enough routing resources for signals on the middle layers. The more layers you give the grid, the better the power but the worse the routability.

3.2 Width, pitch and spacing of stripes

  • Wider stripes: Lower resistance but consume more signal routing tracks.
  • Smaller pitch (denser): Shorter current path to the standard-cell rails, lower IR drop, but increases routing blockage.
  • Interleave VDD and VSS stripes so every cell row point is in close proximity to both rails.
  • Tune using IR-drop simulation maps rather than guessing: start from a reasonable pitch, analyze, then densify locally.

3.3 Rails and via arrays

Standard-cell rails on M1 are connected up to the stripes with via stacks at every intersection. Use multi-cut (via-array) connections where space allows, since single vias are both resistive and EM-prone.

3.4 Macro power

  • Macros usually need their own ring and/or connections from the main stripes to their power pins.
  • Macro pins are on specific metal layers, so the grid must offer a legal connection to those layers.
  • High-power macros (SRAMs, hard IP) deserve extra stripes or direct pad/bump connections.
  • Keep the grid continuous across the macro-free channels; narrow gaps with thin straps are classic IR hot-spots.

3.5 Decoupling capacitors (decaps)

Decaps act as local charge reservoirs that supply current during switching peaks, reducing dynamic IR drop and supply noise. They cost area (and add leakage), so they are placed where dynamic analysis shows need, often late in the flow.

3.6 Multi-voltage and power gating (preview)

Multi-domain designs need separate grids per domain, plus power switches, isolation, retention and level shifters. We will treat this properly in the low-power session.

4. Typical Flow in Practice

1. Connect logical power/ground nets (global net connection) to all cells and macros
2. Define rails (followpins) along standard-cell rows
3. Create rings around the core and around macros that need them
4. Create stripes (vertical + horizontal mesh) on chosen top layers
5. Connect everything: rails to stripes, stripes to rings, rings to pads/bumps, macros to the mesh
6. Run connectivity / DRC checks on the grid (no floating nets, no shorts/opens)
7. Run static IR drop and EM analysis; iterate on width/pitch/layers/vias
8. Later (post-route): sign-off power analysis, dynamic IR, decap insertion

Always verify power connectivity first. A single unconnected macro pin or a stripe that misses its target can masquerade as a catastrophic IR-drop problem, or worse, be silent until LVS fails.

5. Debug Scenarios

🔴 Scenario A: IR drop hot-spot in the middle of a macro-dense region

The static IR drop map shows a red region between two SRAMs, far from any ring, while the rest of the core is within budget.

Walkthrough:
  1. Check if the grid is continuous through the channel: are straps cut by macro blockages, leaving only thin links?
  2. Look at current demand: SRAM macros draw large peak current, and the path from ring to that region may be long.
  3. Fix options: add straps in the channel on a higher layer, add a ring around the macros, increase via count at ring-to-strap junctions, or add additional pad/bump supply nearby if possible.
  4. Re-run analysis; confirm the improvement is local and has not hurt routability.

🔴 Scenario B: Timing fails post-route only in one corner of the chip, STA setup was fine

A cluster of 80 flip-flop endpoints fail setup slack by 400ps in a localized corner.

Walkthrough: Overlay the IR-drop map on the failing paths. If the failing cells sit in a drooping region, they are running at a lower effective voltage than the STA corner assumed. Fix the grid in that region (or run IR-drop-aware timing / apply derate) rather than endlessly resizing cells. Many "mystery" timing failures in one area are really power problems.

🔴 Scenario C: EM violations on a few power stripes and via stacks

Reliability log flags multiple M5/M6 power straps exceeding DC current density limits.

Walkthrough: Identify whether violations are on the wire segment (current density too high → widen or add parallel stripes) or on vias (too few cuts → use via arrays). Check if these stripes are the only path to a high-power block; adding another feed distributes the current. Re-run EM after changes.

🔴 Scenario D: LVS or connectivity check reports floating VDD/VSS on some cells

Post-placement Calibre or Innovus connectivity checker outputs dangling supply pins.

Walkthrough: Likely causes: rails not connected to stripes in an area (stripe missing, or blocked by a keepout), cells added after the power connection step and never connected, or tie-high/tie-low cells and special cells missed by global net connection. Re-run power-net connection, check around blockages and boundaries, then re-verify.

6. Interview Questions

Q1: What is the difference between static and dynamic IR drop?
A: Static uses average current and exposes resistive weakness in the grid. Dynamic uses time-varying switching current and also includes grid capacitance/inductance and decap effects, so it reveals peak droops during simultaneous switching. Static guides grid sizing; dynamic is the sign-off view.
Q2: How does IR drop affect timing?
A: Lower supply voltage at a cell increases its delay. Cells in a droop region are slower than the STA corner assumed, so setup slack erodes; very large droops can also affect hold and clock-tree behaviour.
Q3: Why use the top metal layers for the power grid?
A: Top metals are thicker and wider, so lower resistance per length, which carries current with less drop and better EM margin. They also leave the lower layers for local signal routing.
Q4: What is electromigration and how do you fix violations?
A: Gradual movement of metal atoms under high current density, causing opens or shorts. Fix by widening wires, adding parallel straps, using more via cuts, reducing the current (lower activity/load), or splitting nets.
Q5: Why do decaps reduce dynamic IR drop, and what is their cost?
A: They store charge locally and supply it during switching peaks, so the grid does not have to deliver all the transient current. Cost: area and leakage, so insert them where analysis shows need.
Q6: What is the trade-off in making the power grid denser?
A: Better IR drop and EM, but it consumes routing tracks on the grid layers, so signal routability worsens and congestion rises. The goal is the sparsest grid that meets the IR/EM budget.
Q7: What are followpins / standard-cell rails?
A: The M1 power/ground rails that run along each placement row and connect directly to the cells' supply pins. Because rows alternate orientation, adjacent rows share a rail.
Q8: A macro has IR drop violations at its power pins. What do you check?
A: Number and width of connections from the mesh to the macro pins, via arrays at those junctions, whether the macro has its own ring, the distance to the nearest pad/bump or ring, and the macro's peak current. Then add straps/vias or an additional supply path.

7. Practice Task (Hands-on, 60–90 min)

  1. In OpenLane/OpenROAD, take your floorplanned design and create the power grid with the default stripe pitch and width.
  2. Run the IR-drop analysis (OpenROAD's PDN and static IR tools) and note the worst-case drop and where it occurs.
  3. Halve the stripe pitch (denser) and re-run. Record the change in worst IR drop and in routing congestion later.
  4. Write 4–5 lines: what did the denser grid buy you, and what did it cost?
  5. Bonus: deliberately disconnect one stripe from the ring and observe the IR-drop map, to learn what a connectivity bug looks like.
