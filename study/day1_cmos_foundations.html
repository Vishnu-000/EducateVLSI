Semiconductor Basics — Conductors, Insulators, Semiconductors

Every chip you will ever work on as a Physical Design Engineer is built on a semiconductor — specifically, silicon (Si). To understand why transistors work, you must first understand why silicon is special.

The Three Material Classes

All materials can be classified by how easily electrons move through them:

MaterialResistivity (Ω·cm)ExampleWhy?
Conductor10⁻⁶ to 10⁻³Copper, AluminumOuter electrons are loosely bound — free to move
Semiconductor10⁻³ to 10⁸Silicon, GermaniumElectrons are bound but can be freed with energy or impurities
Insulator10⁸ to 10²²SiO₂, GlassElectrons tightly bound — almost impossible to move
🧠 Analogy
Think of electrons as water in a pipe. A conductor is a wide-open pipe — water flows freely. An insulator is a sealed pipe — nothing flows. A semiconductor is a pipe with a valve — you can control how much flows by adjusting conditions. This controllability is what makes transistors possible.

Silicon's Atomic Structure

Silicon has atomic number 14 — 14 protons, 14 electrons. The electron configuration is 2-8-4. The outermost shell has 4 valence electrons.

In a pure silicon crystal, each silicon atom shares its 4 valence electrons with 4 neighboring atoms, forming covalent bonds. At absolute zero (0 K), all electrons are bound — silicon is an insulator. At room temperature, some bonds break due to thermal energy, freeing electrons to carry charge. But the conductivity is still low — this is the "semi" in semiconductor.

Silicon crystal lattice (simplified 2D view): Si ─── Si ─── Si │ │ │ Si ─── Si ─── Si ← Each line = shared electron pair (covalent bond) │ │ │ Si ─── Si ─── Si At room temp: occasional bond breaks → free electron + hole (positive vacancy) Both electrons AND holes carry current in semiconductors!

Holes — The "Positive Electron"

When a silicon electron breaks free, it leaves behind a hole — a vacancy with a net positive charge. Adjacent electrons can fill this hole, effectively making the hole "move" in the opposite direction of electron flow. In semiconductor physics, holes behave as positive charge carriers with their own mobility.

⚡ PD Relevance

NMOS transistors conduct using electrons (faster). PMOS transistors conduct using holes (slower). This is why, for the same drive strength, PMOS transistors must be roughly 2–2.5× wider than NMOS. PD engineers account for this in cell sizing and layout.

02
Doping — N-type and P-type Silicon

Pure silicon has very limited conductivity. To build transistors, we intentionally introduce impurity atoms — a process called doping. Doping lets us create regions with an excess of either electrons or holes.

N-type Silicon (Donor Doping)

We add elements from Group V of the periodic table — such as Phosphorus (P) or Arsenic (As). These atoms have 5 valence electrons. When they replace a silicon atom in the crystal, 4 electrons form bonds with neighbors. The 5th electron is loosely bound and becomes a free carrier.

Si ─── Si ─── Si │ │ │ Si ─── P ─── Si ← Phosphorus: 5 valence electrons │ │● │ ● = extra free electron Si ─── Si ─── Si Result: Majority carriers = electrons (negative) → N-type Minority carriers = holes

P-type Silicon (Acceptor Doping)

We add elements from Group III — such as Boron (B). These atoms have only 3 valence electrons. When placed in the crystal, only 3 bonds form. The missing 4th bond creates a hole — a free positive carrier.

Si ─── Si ─── Si │ │ │ Si ─── B ─── Si ← Boron: only 3 valence electrons │ ○ │ ○ = hole (missing bond) Si ─── Si ─── Si Result: Majority carriers = holes (positive) → P-type Minority carriers = electrons
PropertyN-typeP-type
Dopant elementPhosphorus, Arsenic (Group V)Boron (Group III)
Extra valence e⁻5 → 1 free electron donated3 → 1 hole created
Majority carriersElectrons (−)Holes (+)
Minority carriersHolesElectrons
Used inNMOS channel, PMOS source/drainPMOS channel, NMOS substrate
📌 Remember This
N-type = Negative carriers dominate (electrons). P-type = Positive carriers dominate (holes). The N and P in NMOS/PMOS directly refer to the type of channel formed between source and drain.
03
The MOSFET — Structure, Operation & Regions

MOSFET = Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor. Every logic cell in a standard cell library — inverter, NAND, NOR, flip-flop — is built from MOSFETs. As a PD engineer, you work with hundreds of millions of them on a single chip.

MOSFET Structure

GATE (G) ← control terminal (metal or poly) │ ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐ │ Gate Oxide │ ← SiO₂ insulator (~1-3nm thick) └─────────────┬─────────────┘ ┌──────┐ │ ┌──────┐ │ N+ │◄─── P-substrate ────────►│ N+ │ ← Source (S) and Drain (D) │(src) │ (channel region) │(drn) │ heavily doped N-type └──────┘ └──────┘ ├────── L ──────┤ ← Channel Length (L) ├──────────── W ────────────┤ ← Channel Width (W) Bulk/Body (B): the P-type substrate (usually tied to GND in NMOS)

The MOSFET has 4 terminals: Gate (G), Drain (D), Source (S), and Body/Bulk (B). The gate is electrically insulated from the channel by a thin oxide layer — this is why the gate draws almost zero current. The gate controls the channel by electric field effect (hence "Field-Effect Transistor").

How the MOSFET Turns ON

For an NMOS transistor (n-channel MOSFET):

  1. Apply positive voltage to Gate (VGS > 0)
  2. The electric field from the gate repels holes from the P-substrate below the oxide
  3. It attracts electrons (minority carriers) to the surface
  4. When VGS exceeds threshold voltage (Vt), enough electrons accumulate to form a conducting inversion layer = the channel
  5. Electrons can now flow from Source to Drain — transistor is ON
🧠 Analogy
The gate is like a magnet held above a pile of iron filings (electrons) mixed with wood chips (holes). When you bring the magnet close, iron filings rush up and form a path. That path is the conducting channel. Remove the magnet — filings scatter — channel disappears — transistor OFF.

Threshold Voltage (Vt)

Vt is the minimum gate voltage needed to form the channel and turn the transistor ON. This is one of the most important parameters in PD:

  • Low Vt (LVT): turns on easily, fast switching, high leakage → used for critical timing paths
  • Standard Vt (SVT): balanced — default for most logic
  • High Vt (HVT): needs more voltage to turn on, slower, very low leakage → used for non-critical paths to save power
⚡ PD Engineer Relevance

Multi-Vt optimization is a key PD technique. After routing, PD engineers "ECO" (change) cells from SVT to HVT in non-critical paths to dramatically reduce leakage power — sometimes by 30–50%. Knowing WHY this works (Vt physics) makes you a better engineer.

Three Operating Regions of MOSFET

RegionConditionBehaviorUsed As
Cutoff (OFF)VGS < VtNo channel, no current (except tiny leakage)Switch OFF
Linear (Triode)VGS > Vt, VDS < VGS−VtTransistor behaves like a resistor — current ∝ VDSLogic transition
SaturationVGS > Vt, VDS ≥ VGS−VtCurrent nearly constant (independent of VDS)Amplifiers, current source
📌 PD Relevance
In digital CMOS logic, transistors are either fully OFF (cutoff) or fully in linear region (acting as a low-resistance switch). Saturation occurs briefly during switching transitions, which is when short-circuit current flows and heat is generated.

NMOS vs PMOS — The Complementary Pair

CMOS technology uses both NMOS and PMOS transistors together. Understanding their differences — and why we need both — is fundamental to understanding every logic gate.

🧠 Analogy
NMOS is like a normally-open door — electricity (a person) can only push it open by applying force (high voltage). PMOS is like a spring-loaded door — it stays open by default and closes when you apply force (high voltage). Together, when one opens, the other closes. This push-pull action is the essence of CMOS.

Why Not Use Just NMOS?

Pure NMOS logic (NMOS-only gates with resistive loads) was used in early chips (Intel 4004). It has two fatal flaws:

  • DC current always flows — even when output is stable, current draws from VDD to GND through the resistive load → massive static power
  • Weak pull-up — resistor pull-up is slow, limiting speed

CMOS solves both: complementary transistors ensure no DC path at steady state, and the PMOS actively pulls up while NMOS actively pulls down — full rail-to-rail swing with near-zero static power.

05
CMOS Inverter — Construction, Operation & Power

The CMOS inverter is the most fundamental logic gate and the basic unit of analysis for timing, power, and noise in CMOS. Every complex gate can be analyzed by breaking it into inverter-equivalent stages.

Circuit Construction

VDD (e.g., 1.0V) │ ┌────┴────┐ │ PMOS │ ← Source tied to VDD │ │ Gate connected to Input └────┬────┘ │ IN ──────────┼──────── OUT ← Output node │ ┌────┴────┐ │ NMOS │ ← Source tied to GND │ │ Gate connected to Input └────┬────┘ │ GND (0V) Both transistors share the same gate input and drive the same output node. CL = load capacitance at output (wire + next gate input)

Operation: Case-by-Case Analysis

Case 1: Input = Logic 0 (0V)

  • NMOS gate = 0V → VGS_NMOS = 0V < Vt → NMOS is OFF (open circuit to GND)
  • PMOS gate = 0V → VGS_PMOS = 0 − VDD = −VDD < −|Vt| → PMOS is ON (connects output to VDD)
  • Output charges up to VDD → Output = Logic 1 ✅
  • DC current path: VDD → PMOS → Output. NMOS OFF. No current to GND.

Case 2: Input = Logic 1 (VDD)

  • NMOS gate = VDD → VGS_NMOS = VDD > Vt → NMOS is ON (connects output to GND)
  • PMOS gate = VDD → VGS_PMOS = VDD − VDD = 0 > −|Vt| → PMOS is OFF (open circuit)
  • Output discharges to GND → Output = Logic 0 ✅
  • DC current path: Output → NMOS → GND. PMOS OFF. No current from VDD.

Case 3: During Transition (Input switching 0→1)

  • For a brief moment, both PMOS and NMOS are partially ON
  • A short-circuit current briefly flows from VDD to GND
  • This is one component of dynamic power — minimized by fast transitions (steep input edges)

The Voltage Transfer Characteristic (VTC)

The VTC plots Output voltage vs Input voltage and shows how the inverter transitions between states:

Vout │ VDD │─────────\ │ \ │ \ ← Transition region (gain > 1) │ \ 0 │─────────────\──────────── Vin 0 VIL Vth VIH VDD VIL = Maximum input voltage recognized as Logic 0 VIH = Minimum input voltage recognized as Logic 1 Vth = Switching threshold (≈ VDD/2 for matched CMOS)

Power Dissipation in CMOS Inverter

Understanding power in the inverter gives you the formula that governs ALL of CMOS power:

P_total = P_dynamic + P_short_circuit + P_leakage

Dynamic Power (Switching Power)

P_dynamic = α · C_L · V²_DD · f
  • α = activity factor (0 to 1) — fraction of clock cycles where output switches. A register toggling every cycle: α = 1. A rarely-switching wire: α = 0.01
  • C_L = load capacitance = output wire capacitance + input capacitance of driven gates
  • V_DD = supply voltage. This appears SQUARED — reducing VDD by 20% cuts dynamic power by 36%
  • f = clock frequency
🧠 Analogy
Every time output switches from 0→1, you're charging the load capacitor from GND to VDD. Energy stored = ½·C·V². When it switches 1→0, that energy is discharged as heat through the NMOS. You spend ½·C·V² per transition, and with two transitions per cycle (0→1 and 1→0), total energy per cycle = C·V². At frequency f: P = C·V²·f. The activity factor α accounts for the fact that not every cycle causes a transition.

Leakage Power (Static Power)

Even when the inverter isn't switching, transistors leak tiny currents because no transistor is perfectly OFF in the subthreshold region. This is dominant in advanced nodes (28nm, 14nm, 7nm, 5nm) and the reason battery life suffers even when your phone screen is off.

⚡ Critical PD Insight

The V²_DD dependence means voltage is the most powerful lever: cutting VDD from 1.0V to 0.7V reduces dynamic power by 51%. This is why every advanced SoC uses multiple voltage domains — high performance blocks run at 1.0V, memory at 0.9V, always-on blocks at 0.7V. PD engineers implement multi-voltage domains with special cells (level shifters, isolation cells). You'll implement this in Phase 6.

06
CMOS Logic Gates — NAND, NOR & Complex Gates

Now that you understand the inverter, you can build ANY logic gate using one simple rule: the Complementary Topology Rule.

The Golden Rule of CMOS Gate Design

Every CMOS gate has two networks connected to the output: Pull-Up Network (PUN) of PMOS transistors connected to VDD, and Pull-Down Network (PDN) of NMOS transistors connected to GND. PUN and PDN are always complementary duals of each other: series NMOS ↔ parallel PMOS, and parallel NMOS ↔ series PMOS.

NAND Gate (Most Common Gate in CMOS)

NAND = NOT AND. Output = 0 ONLY when ALL inputs are 1.

📌 Why NAND is preferred over AND
A NAND gate requires fewer transistors than AND (AND = NAND + INV = 4+2=6 transistors, 2-input NAND = 4 transistors). NAND is also faster — its series NMOS stack is smaller. Synthesis tools map most logic into NAND/NOR trees. As a PD engineer, you'll see NAND cells (NAND2, NAND3, NAND4) everywhere in your netlists.

NOR Gate

NOR = NOT OR. Output = 0 when ANY input is 1. PDN: NMOS in PARALLEL. PUN: PMOS in SERIES.

📌 NOR vs NAND Speed Comparison
NOR is slower than NAND. Why? PMOS transistors (used in NOR's pull-up series stack) have lower hole mobility (~3× slower than electrons in NMOS). Series PMOS in NOR means the pull-up path is very weak. NAND's series NMOS (electrons) is faster. This is why synthesis tools prefer NAND-based implementations.

Complex Gates: AOI and OAI

Real chips rarely use simple 2-input gates. Complex gates combine AND-OR-INVERT (AOI) or OR-AND-INVERT (OAI) in a single cell, reducing transistor count and delay.

AOI21 Gate: Out = NOT(A·B + C)

VDD │ ┌─────┴─────┐ [PC] [PB]─[PA] ← C PMOS alone, AB PMOS in series └─────┬─────┘ │ OUT │ [NC] ← C alone │ ┌─────┴─────┐ ← AB in parallel with C [NA] [NB] └─────┬─────┘ │ GND Function: OUT = NOT(A AND B OR C)

AOI/OAI complex gates are heavily used in high-performance paths because they implement 2–3 logic operations with the delay of roughly one inverter stage. PD engineers see cells named AOI21, AOI22, OAI21, OAI211 frequently in netlists.

Voltage Levels, Noise Margin & Signal Integrity

In the real world, signals are never perfect 0V or 1V. Wires pick up noise from neighboring switching wires (crosstalk), power supply fluctuates (IR drop), and transistors have finite gain. Noise Margin defines how much noise a signal can absorb and still be correctly interpreted.

The Four Critical Voltage Parameters

Vout / Vin │ VDD │══════════════════════════════ (supply = 1.0V example) │ VOH │─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ Minimum output HIGH (e.g. 0.85V) │ ████████████████████████████ │ ████ Valid HIGH output ██████ ← Gates must output within this zone VIH │─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ Min input guaranteed read as HIGH (e.g. 0.70V) │ │ ░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░ │ ░░░░ FORBIDDEN ZONE ░░░░░░░░ ← Undefined behavior. Avoid at all costs. │ ░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░░ │ VIL │─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ Max input guaranteed read as LOW (e.g. 0.30V) │ ████████████████████████████ │ ████ Valid LOW output ███████ ← Gates must output within this zone VOL │─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ Maximum output LOW (e.g. 0.15V) │ 0 │══════════════════════════════ GND = 0V

Noise Margins — Defined

NM_HIGH = VOH − VIH   (how much noise a HIGH signal can absorb) NM_LOW = VIL − VOL   (how much noise a LOW signal can absorb)

For the example values above:

  • NM_HIGH = 0.85 − 0.70 = 0.15V = 150mV
  • NM_LOW = 0.30 − 0.15 = 0.15V = 150mV

This means: if a wire carrying a HIGH signal gets hit by up to 150mV of noise, the receiving gate still correctly reads it as HIGH. Larger noise margin = more robust design.

What Degrades Noise Margin in PD?

EffectWhat HappensPD Solution

⚡ Why PD Engineers Care About Noise Margin

When you analyze Signal Integrity in routing (Phase 5), you're essentially checking: "Can this net's noise margin absorb the crosstalk induced by its neighbors?" If not → functional failure. You fix it by increasing wire spacing, adding shielding, or rerouting. The physics behind it all starts right here.

Slew Rate (Rise/Fall Time)

Slew rate = how fast a signal transitions from LOW to HIGH (rise) or HIGH to LOW (fall). Measured in V/ns or expressed as transition time (10%–90% of swing).

  • Slow slew: signal spends more time in the forbidden zone → longer period where a noise glitch could cause misinterpretation. Also increases short-circuit current during transition.
  • Fast slew: good for signal integrity, but generates more switching noise (higher dI/dt → inductive L·di/dt bounce on power supply)
  • Target: slew within a specified range — typically defined in SDC as max_transition constraints
📌 In Timing Reports
Every timing path report shows a "slew" or "transition" column. If slew exceeds the max_transition constraint on a net, PD tools insert buffers to fix it. You'll see this constantly when running post-route STA. Now you know the physics behind why it matters.

Day 1 — What to Remember Forever
Semiconductors
Silicon's 4 valence electrons form covalent bonds. Doping with Group V → N-type (electron majority). Group III → P-type (hole majority).
MOSFET
Voltage-controlled switch. Gate field creates/destroys the channel. Vt = minimum gate voltage to turn ON. Three regions: cutoff, linear, saturation.
NMOS vs PMOS
NMOS: HIGH gate = ON, source to GND, electron carriers. PMOS: LOW gate = ON, source to VDD, hole carriers. PMOS ~2-3× slower → sized wider.
CMOS Inverter
PMOS on top (VDD), NMOS on bottom (GND). At steady state: one is always OFF → zero DC current → near-zero static power. Power only on switching.
Dynamic Power
P = α·C·V²·f. The V² factor is key — voltage is the most powerful knob. Reducing VDD from 1.0V to 0.7V cuts dynamic power by 51%.
Gate Design Rule
Series NMOS ↔ parallel PMOS (NAND). Parallel NMOS ↔ series PMOS (NOR). PDN and PUN are always complementary duals.
Noise Margin
NM_H = VOH − VIH. NM_L = VIL − VOL. IR drop reduces VOH → shrinks NM_H. Crosstalk adds noise. Slow slew = longer forbidden zone exposure.
Multi-Vt
High-Vt cells = slower but less leakage. Low-Vt = faster but more leakage. PD engineers swap cells to trade timing slack for power savings.
